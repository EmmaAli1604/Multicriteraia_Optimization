Introduction

The solution of a decision problem consists in choosing a “good” or “best” alternative from a set of alternatives, assuming the existence of certain criteria according to which the quality of the alternatives is measured.

Decision problems with a countable number of alternatives are called discrete; others are called continuous.

Discrete problems have finitely many alternatives that are explicitly known, and the goal is to select the most preferred one. Multicriteria decision aid deals with such problems.

Discrete vs. Continuous

1.1 Optimization with Multiple Criteria

Discrete Problems

Discrete problems are decision problems characterized by a countable number of alternatives. The set of alternatives may be either explicitly known or implicitly described through constraints in the form of mathematical functions.

Continuous Problems

In continuous problems, the set of alternatives allows for infinitely many different choices. The feasible set of alternatives is generally given through mathematical constraints, such as functions optimized over the nonnegative real line.

The nonnegative real line is \(\mathbb{R}_+ = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \geq 0\}\).

1.2 Decision Space and Objective (Criterion) Space

This section introduces the fundamental notions of the decision (or variable) space and the criterion (or objective) space, which contain the alternatives and their images under the objective function mappings, respectively. In other words, the decision space is the space of the variable \(x\), and the criterion space is the space of \(y = f(x)\): the decision space is the one that varies, while the criterion (objective) space is the image obtained from it.

Feasible set: the set \(X\), of which every alternative is an element, is called the feasible set, and the space of which \(X\) is a subset is called the decision space.

The image of \(X\) under \(f = (f_1, f_2)\) is denoted:

\[Y := f(X) := \{ y \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y = f(x) \text{ for some } x \in X \}.\]

The image of \(X\) depends on the number of objective functions under consideration, which determines the dimension of the criterion space in which it lies. \(Y\) is referred to as the image of the feasible set, or the feasible set in criterion space. The space from which the criterion values are taken is called the criterion space.

The aspect of decision making concerned with supporting decision makers in selecting a final solution from a set of mathematically “equally optimal” solutions is often referred to as multicriteria decision aid (MCDA).

Definitions

Definition (Decision Space, Criterion Space, Objective Functions, Nondominated Points, MCDA)

  • Decision space: the space that contains the feasible set \(X\) of alternatives; also called the variable space.
  • Criterion space: the space that contains the image \(Y = f(X)\) of the feasible set under the objective functions; also called the objective space.
  • Objective functions: the functions \(f_1, \dots, f_p\) that map each alternative \(x \in X\) to a vector of criterion values \(f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^p\).
  • Nondominated points: points \(y \in Y\) for which no other \(y' \in Y\) exists that is at least as good as \(y\) in every component and strictly better in at least one.
  • Multicriteria decision aid (MCDA): the discipline concerned with supporting decision makers in choosing a final, most preferred solution among a set of mathematically equally optimal (efficient) alternatives.

1.3 Notions of Optimality

We now introduce, informally, the concept of “minimization” in multicriteria optimization problems:

\[\min_{x \in X} \big(f_1(x), \dots, f_p(x)\big)\]

The fundamental importance of efficiency (Pareto optimality) is based on the observation that any \(x\) which is not efficient cannot represent a most preferred alternative for a decision maker, because there exists at least one other feasible solution \(x' \in X\) such that \(f_k(x') \leq f_k(x)\) for all \(k = 1, \dots, p\), with strict inequality holding for at least one \(k\). In that case, \(x'\) should clearly be preferred to \(x\).

All the notions of optimality above are, for now, represented only informally; they will be made precise in the following sections.

1.4 Orders and Cones

Orders and Preorders

In order to define the meaning of “min”, we must specify how objective function vectors \((f_1(x), \dots, f_p(x))\) are to be compared for different alternatives \(x \in X\). The different ways of doing this arise from the fact that, for \(p \geq 2\), there is no canonical order on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) as there is on \(\mathbb{R}\). Therefore, weaker notions of order must be used.

Let \(S\) be any set. A binary relation on \(S\) is a subset \(R\) of \(S \times S\). We introduce some properties of binary relations below.

Definition (Properties of a Binary Relation)

A binary relation \(R\) on \(S\) is called:

  • reflexive if \((s,s) \in R\) for all \(s \in S\)
  • irreflexive if \((s,s) \notin R\) for all \(s \in S\)
  • symmetric if \((s^1,s^2) \in R \implies (s^2,s^1) \in R\) for all \(s^1, s^2 \in S\)
  • asymmetric if \((s^1,s^2) \in R \implies (s^2,s^1) \notin R\) for all \(s^1, s^2 \in S\)
  • antisymmetric if \((s^1,s^2) \in R\) and \((s^2,s^1) \in R \implies s^1 = s^2\), for all \(s^1, s^2 \in S\)
  • transitive if \((s^1,s^2) \in R\) and \((s^2,s^3) \in R \implies (s^1,s^3) \in R\) for all \(s^1, s^2, s^3 \in S\)
  • negatively transitive if \((s^1,s^2) \notin R\) and \((s^2,s^3) \notin R \implies (s^1,s^3) \notin R\) for all \(s^1, s^2, s^3 \in S\)
  • connected if \((s^1,s^2) \in R\) or \((s^2,s^1) \in R\) for all \(s^1 \neq s^2\) in \(S\)
  • strongly connected (or total) if \((s^1,s^2) \in R\) or \((s^2,s^1) \in R\) for all \(s^1, s^2 \in S\)

Definition (Equivalence Relation and Preorder)

A binary relation \(R\) on a set \(S\) is:

  • an equivalence relation if it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive
  • a preorder (quasi-order) if it is reflexive and transitive

If \(R\) is a preorder, the pair \((S,R)\) is called a preordered set. In the context of (pre)orders, a more convenient notation for the relation is often used: \(s^1 \preceq s^2\) as shorthand for \((s^1,s^2) \in R\), and \(s^2 \not\preceq s^1\) for \((s^2,s^1) \notin R\), referring indiscriminately to the relation \(R\) or the relation \(\preceq\). This notation can be read as “preferred to.”

Given any preorder \(\preceq\), two other relations are closely associated with \(\preceq\). We define them as follows: * \(s^1 \prec s^2 \iff s^1 \preceq s^2 \text{ and } s^2 \not\preceq s^1\) * \(s^1 \sim s^2 \iff s^1 \preceq s^2 \text{ and } s^2 \preceq s^1\)

Actually, \(\prec\) and \(\sim\) can be seen as the strict preference and equivalence (or indifference) relation, respectively, associated with the preference defining preorder \(\preceq\).

Proposition 1.6

Let \(\preceq\) be a preorder on \(S\). Then relation \(\prec\) defined above is irreflexive and transitive and relation \(\sim\) defined above is an equivalence relation.

Proof.

Proposition 1.7

An asymmetric binary relation is irreflexive. A transitive, irreflexive binary relation is asymmetric.

Proof.

Proposition 1.8

A binary relation \(\preceq\) on \(S\) is:

  • a total preorder if it is reflexive, transitive and connected,
  • a total order if it is an antisymmetric total preorder, (so this means that it has to be antisymmetric and a total preorder)
  • a strict weak order if it is asymmetric and negatively transitive.

Proof.

Proposition 1.9

If \(\preceq\) is a total preorder on \(S\), then the associated relation \(\prec\) is a strict weak order. If \(\prec\) is a strict weak order on \(S\), then \(\preceq\) defined by:

\[s^1 \preceq s^2 \iff \text{either } s^1 \prec s^2 \text{ or } (s^1 \not\prec s^2 \text{ and } s^2 \not\prec s^1)\]

is a total preorder.

Proof.

Most important classes of relations in multicriteria optimization - partial orders and strict partial orders are introduced now.

Definition 1.10

A binary relation \(\preceq\) is called:

  • partial order if it is reflexive, transitive and antisymmetric,
  • strict partial order if it is asymmetric and transitive (or, equivalently if it is irreflexive and transitive)

Let \(y^1, y^2 \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and if \(y^1 \neq y^2\) let \(k^* := \min\{k: y^1_k \neq y^2_k\}\).

With the (weak, strict) componentwise orders, we define subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) as follows: * \(\mathbb{R}_{\geqq}^p := \{y \in \mathbb{R}^p: y \geqq 0\}\), the nonnegative orthant of \(\mathbb{R}^p\). * \(\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^p := \{y \in \mathbb{R}^p : y \geq 0\} = \mathbb{R}_{\geqq}^p \setminus \{0\}\). * \(\mathbb{R}_{>}^p := \{y \in \mathbb{R}^p: y > 0\} = \text{int } \mathbb{R}_{\geqq}^p\), the positive orthant of \(\mathbb{R}^p\).

Note that for \(p=1\) we have \(\mathbb{R}_{\geq} = \mathbb{R}_{>}\)

Cones

Definition 1.11

A subset \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) is called a cone if \(\alpha d \in C\) for all \(d \in C\) and for all \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\) where \(\alpha > 0\).

In other words the cone represent a formula where the vector is multiplied by a constant which has to be greater than 0.

Let \(S, S_1, S_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) and \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\). We denote by:

\[\alpha S := \{\alpha s : s \in S\}\]

especially, \(-S := \{-s : s \in S\}\). The algebraic sum of \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) is:

\[S_1 + S_2 := \{s^1 + s^2 : s^1 \in S_1, s^2 \in S_2\}\]

If \(S_1 = \{s\}\) is a singleton, we also write \(s + S_2\) instead of \(\{s\} + S_2\).

More notation, for \(S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) or \(S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\):

Definition 1.12

  • \(\text{int}(S)\) is the interior of \(S\),
  • \(\text{ri}(S)\) is the relative interior of \(S\),
  • \(\text{bd}(S)\) is the boundary of \(S\),
  • \(\text{cl}(S) = \text{int}(S) \cup \text{bd}(S)\) is the closure of \(S\)
  • \(\text{conv}(S)\) is the convex hull of \(S\)

Definition 1.13

A cone \(C\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is called:

  • nontrivial or proper if \(C \neq \emptyset\) and \(C \neq \mathbb{R}^n\),
  • convex if \(\alpha d^1 + (1-\alpha)d^2 \in C\) for all \(d^1, d^2 \in C\) and for all \(0 < \alpha < 1\),
  • pointed if for \(d \in C\) and \(-d \in C \implies d=0\), i.e., \(C \cap (-C) \subseteq \{0\}\).

Due to the definition of a cone, \(C\) is convex if for all \(d^1, d^2 \in C\) we have \(d^1 + d^2 \in C\), too: \(\alpha d^1\) and \((1-\alpha)d^2 \in C\) because \(C\) is a cone. Therefore closedness of a \(C\) under addition is sufficient for convexity. Then, using the algebraic sum, we can say that \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) is a convex cone if \(\alpha C \subseteq C\) for all \(\alpha > 0\) and \(C + C \subseteq C\).
