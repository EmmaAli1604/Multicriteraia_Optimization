Introduction

To further analyze the subject of Smart Predict+Optimize in the context of inventory management, we need to look at a replicable use case for our problem. Exploring the IEEE competition on Predict+Optimize will give us the correct context.

Optimization problems that must be solved over an unknown future are at the core of many complex real-world operations — supply chaining, inventory management, and staffing rosters, among others. In other words, this applies to everything that involves predicting uncertainty while pursuing multiple objectives. In particular, for this use case, it is important to optimize the solution in light of the global transition to reduce CO₂ emissions.

Renewable energy production is characterized by variability over time, which means it correlates closely with time-series optimization. Demand needs to be predicted and scheduled to make the best use of available supply, with energy storage systems (such as batteries) scheduled optimally to cover shortfalls — all based on unknown future production and demand.

One way to address this uncertainty is through robust optimization or stochastic optimization, using probabilistic forecasts as inputs instead of point forecasts.

Forecasting is often a precursor to optimization, which typically pursues objectives such as minimizing costs, maximizing renewable energy use, or ensuring energy system stability.

Having accurate forecasting is crucial, because it provides the necessary inputs for the optimization model. Without it, the optimization process may rely on incorrect or incomplete information.

Predict + Optimize

Now that we have some context on the importance of accurate forecasting and its optimization, it is time to introduce a new concept: Predict+Optimize, where forecasting and optimization are not treated as isolated tasks, but their interaction is taken into account.

Using this approach, forecasts are chosen and evaluated based on their contribution to the actual downstream cost of the optimization problem, in preference to arbitrary forecast measures such as:

The IEEE-CIS Technical Challenge

All of these concepts inspired the competition we will discuss: the IEEE-CIS Technical Challenge on Predict+Optimize for Renewable Energy Scheduling. The goal of the challenge was to provide a relevant real-world dataset and optimization benchmark problem, along with strong baseline solutions, to establish a state of the art in the area for the research community.

The main problem originates from the Monash Net Zero Initiative, which involves a campus-wide microgrid equipped with rooftop solar photovoltaic installations and battery storage. This system operates across the entire university campus, with the goal of achieving net-zero emissions by 2030. The university’s objectives are to:

  1. Maximize self-consumption of electricity.
  2. Participate in energy demand response programs.
  3. Keep track of electricity price and yearly peak load tariffs to manage costs.

Forecasting

During phase 1 of the competition, participants were expected to predict the power demand of the 6 buildings and the power production of the 6 arrays of solar panels over 15-minute intervals for each day. The actual energy demand of each building and the power production of each array of solar panels were then released. In phase 2, participants were expected to provide the 15-minute energy demand and production forecasts for the same buildings and solar panels for the 30 days of November 2022.

For this section, we are going to analyze the 1st place and 7th place forecasting solutions.

1st Place Solution

The best solution implemented an ensemble of LightGBM, a tree-based algorithm, using calendar features and dynamic features (lags, mean, standard deviation), combined with ensembling models that use daily and weekly weather features.

Their choice of training-set size per series, combined with the optimization approach, produced a methodology that is highly generalizable and robust. They explored several cases and searched for their impact — for example, the time series during COVID provided a great deal of data, making the forecasting horizon diverse across multiple scenarios. On the other hand, the correlation between solar power and buildings’ demand power is highly dependent on weather conditions; the hourly weather data provided by the competition organizers was used for this purpose.

Various statistical models — ARIMA, Random Forest (RF), LightGBM, and SVR — were developed and used for the building predictive models. Although RF and SVR were fairly accurate and competitive with LightGBM, LightGBM was ultimately chosen since it is significantly faster and returns reliable forecasts.

The hyperparameters were also optimized, and the most significant features for each model were selected.

Optimization

The main problem was to create a timetable for a set of activities over the coming month, across the set of 6 buildings, with the objective of minimizing the total electricity cost.

Each building was specified by a number of large and small rooms available. Solar production attributable to each building varied between scenarios by assigning one of the six solar time series to the building in each instance.

Activities were specified by the number of rooms required, whether those rooms were large or small, the duration of the activity, the energy load of that activity, and whether it was recurrent or one-off. The battery specifications were matched to be close to the actual scale and performance of two batteries currently in the Monash microgrid. Each battery was characterized by its capacity, maximum charge/discharge power, and round-trip efficiency.

The objective function vector consisted of:

  1. The wholesale energy cost of all energy imported.
  2. A peak load demand charge.
  3. The additional profit of scheduled once-off activities.

More generally, the constraints imposed by the problem include:

  • Room availability
  • Precedence constraints
  • Battery constraints
  • Activity timing
  • Energy balance

Conceptually, the activity scheduling problem thus defined is an instance of the Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problem (RCPSP).

What is the RCPSP?

It is a well-known problem in operations research, where the goal is to schedule a set of project activities within given resource constraints and time windows. Because recurring activities with precedence must be scheduled on different days, the problem also exhibits minimum time lags — the shortest allowable time between the start of one activity and the start of another — as well as maximum time lags.

For this section, we are going to analyze the 1st place and 7th place optimization solutions.

1st Place Solution

In the optimization component, the team reviewed multiple scenarios using sample average approximation, which minimizes the average cost across multiple scenarios. This approach appears reproducible and solid, giving it strong points in terms of robustness.

The objective function for minimizing the total energy cost includes the square of the maximum load. They used a linearization technique to linearize the objective function and developed a mixed-integer linear program that captures all the constraints of the problem.

One of the input parameters of the optimization model is the net base load, which is the difference between the total predicted base load of the buildings and the generation of their solar panels, per time slot.

Their optimization proposal does not rely on a single forecast. Decision-making under uncertainty plays a crucial role in managing energy systems and must be addressed properly. Their proposal was to treat the net base loads at each time step as a random variable within the optimization model, using the so-called Sample Average Approximation Method (SAAM) — an optimization approach that minimizes the average cost of a solution across multiple scenarios (predictive outcomes), rather than relying on just one.

How to Evaluate the Forecast?

The metric used in this case was MASE (Mean Absolute Scaled Error), a commonly used error measure for forecast evaluation, defined as follows:

\[ \text{MASE} = \frac{1}{h} \sum_{k=1}^{h} \frac{|Y_k - F_k|}{\dfrac{1}{M-S} \displaystyle\sum_{t=S+1}^{M} |Y_t - Y_{t-S}|} \]

where \(M\) is the number of instances in the training series, \(S\) is the length of the seasonal cycle of the dataset, \(h\) is the forecast horizon, \(F_k\) are the forecasts, and \(Y_k\) are the actual values. MASE was calculated individually for each time series and averaged for the final error used to rank the submissions.

How to Evaluate the Optimization?

For the optimization evaluation, two components must be taken into account:

Feasibility

The schedules were required to assign a time period to every recurring activity while adhering to the following constraints, for every activity \(a_i\):

  • The starting period must fall within the week containing the first Monday of the month.
  • Start time \(\geq 9\).
  • Finish time (start time + activity duration) \(\leq 17\).
  • Activity precedence must be observed.
  • Every battery schedule must respect the battery’s capacity.

Objective

For a feasible schedule, the objective value is computed in terms of the cost of the schedule. The main objective is to minimize the scheduling cost using an objective function \(O\).

Conclusions

By establishing a well-defined, reproducible problem with a realistic setting, the challenge organizers hoped to encourage further methodological developments and comparative evaluations in this domain. For a decision-making project, the main goals are therefore that it must be reproducible and based on a realistic setting.

This competition demonstrated that forecasting and optimization cannot be treated as two isolated components; however, the nuanced interplay between probabilistic forecasting and optimization-based decision-making still requires deeper exploration.

The winning team used stochastic optimization to minimize the expected cost across a number of forecast scenarios.

In the end, learning how to correctly implement Predict+Optimize algorithms helps us understand how to make better decisions based on forecasting results. Generally, for predicting time series, the most common models are tree-based algorithms such as Random Forest, LightGBM, or XGBoost. Based on the probabilistic results from these models, stochastic algorithms are then used for the multicriteria optimization and the objective function vectors — helping us learn how to select the most efficient solution from the solution set, based on the different objectives being pursued.
